When I teach my Audio Engineering class, in&nbsp;the very first lesson I always ask my students what is sound for them. The answers are always interesting because&nbsp;_sound_ is something that we all experience every day:&nbsp;we are surrounded by it&nbsp;but, nonetheless, we struggle to find the right words to describe it. > Sound is kinda like a color that you can hear.

I love this quote because most of the time we refer to sound using visual images. If you think about it,&nbsp;it's&nbsp;hard to describe&nbsp;_colours&nbsp;_too.
## So... What is sound?
Sound is caused by _vibrations of molecules in any object (or medium)_. For instance, we can hear sound in water or in other materials such as metals but, because we are used to hear sounds in an environment composed mainly of air, we can safely say that&nbsp;_sound is the vibration of air._ Air is a medium composed by molecules of various type: oxygen, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, etc... but we don't want (and not need to) to think about it. The real thing that matters is that between these molecules there is empty space. This mean that we can push together (or compress) air molecules into a smaller place. Thanks to &nbsp;the elasticity property of air, after being compressed, these molecules&nbsp;spring apart. Unfortunately air molecules are so small that we can't see this phenomenon happening in the air, however, as shown in the video below, we can reproduce it with&nbsp;a slinky spring: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7cDAYFTXq3E Sound travels&nbsp;in the air through _sound waves_ which are longitudinal waves that are generated when the molecules are pushed together ( **compression** ),&nbsp;then allowed to spring apart ( **rarefaction** ) and then pushed together again in a repeating pattern. [![Sound Wave](/assets/uploads/2015/05/wavepulse.gif)](http://www.acs.psu.edu/drussell/Demos/waves-intro/waves-intro.html) Image courtesy of Daniel A. Russell As shown in this animation, when pushed together, molecules don't move really&nbsp;far from their original position even though sound waves can travel long distances. As reference, we usually say that in the air&nbsp;sound travels at approximately&nbsp; **340 meters per second** but,&nbsp;as you can easily guess, this&nbsp;value can vary greatly due to the density of the molecules which is affected by temperature, humidity and, of course,&nbsp;altitude.

> In space no one can hear you scream

Do you remember this&nbsp;tagline used to promote Alien's movie in 1979?&nbsp;Even if space isn't empty, the distances between molecules are so large that&nbsp;the repeating pattern of compressions and rarefaction described above can't be generated so&nbsp;there can be no sound.
## How do we perceive sound?
Back to the earth, we just learned that sound waves travels through air until they reach our ears.&nbsp;The differences in air pressure are captured by the outer ear (pinna) and funneled into the ear canal. The _eardrum_ – the limit between the outer and the middle ear –&nbsp;starts to vibrate and transmits this movement&nbsp;to three small bones: _the hammer, the anvil and the stirrup_.&nbsp;Eventually the vibrations reach the fluid inside the _cochlea_ in the inner ear.&nbsp;The vibrations of the fluid are captured by tiny hair cells that with their movement, generate electrical signals which are picked up by the hearing nerve and sent to the brain. In the end is the brain to translate the electrical pulses into what we perceive as sound. If you think about it, the ear is a very sensitive organ: if we are staying in a very quiet room with no sound coming from the outside we can easily ear a mosquito flying around 3 meters away. Likewise our ears&nbsp;are able to stand&nbsp;large amount of pressure variations like&nbsp;a jet engine at full power for a limited time before being damaged. We have to keep in mind though that we don't all hear&nbsp;the same:&nbsp;there are many factors that can alter our perception of sounds:&nbsp;our age, our medical history, if we've been exposed to very loud sounds for long periods of time and so on.
